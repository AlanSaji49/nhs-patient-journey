The NHS patient journey

How adult patients enter, move through and leave an NHS acute hospital. The themes are the same whatever the specialty or presentation. Explore the map, then follow two patients through it.

Scope: adult acute and elective pathways only. Paediatric, maternity and mental health pathways are excluded.

Chart B · Discharge from the wards

What has to happen before a ward patient can leave. Discharge waits for the slowest stream.

Step 1

Medical review

The patient is deemed medically optimised for discharge (MOFD) by the consultant / medical team.

Then all streams run in parallel

Medical

Doctors (usually FY1/FY2)

  • Discharge summary to GP
  • Follow-up arranged: clinic, bloods, pending results

Therapy

Some patients

Physiotherapists and occupational therapists

  • Functional assessment: mobility, stairs, activities of daily living
  • Equipment ordered and delivered
  • Outcome: home, home with package of care, rehab, or placement

Pharmacy

Pharmacists and pharmacy technicians

  • Medicines reconciliation and discharge prescription screened
  • Take-home medicines (TTOs) dispensed
  • Patient counselling on new medicines

Nursing

Ward nurses

  • Discharge checklist: cannula out, obs stable, wounds dressed
  • Nursing discharge letter and community referrals, e.g. district nurses
  • Patient and family told the plan

Social care

When needed

Social workers and discharge coordinators / flow team

  • Package of care or placement arranged
  • Delayed patients tracked
  • Capacity or safeguarding decisions where relevant

Logistics

Patient transport crews (or family) and porters

  • Transport arranged
  • Move to the discharge lounge to free the bed

Chart C · Short-form discharge

ED, SDEC, AMU/SAU and day case. No MOFD step: the decision is whether the patient is safe to go home.

Step 1

Clinical decision

The patient is assessed and safe to go home.

  • ED, SDEC, AMU, SAU: decided by an ED doctor, advanced clinical practitioner (ACP) or acute medical/surgical team, often with senior / consultant review
  • Day case: decided by the anaesthetist or surgeon against recovery criteria (awake, pain controlled, eating and drinking, passed urine)
Then, in parallel

Medical

Doctors / ACPs (day case: surgeon)

  • Discharge letter to GP
  • Safety-netting advice: when to return
  • Follow-up arranged if needed: clinic, GP, results

Pharmacy

Not always needed

Doctors prescribe, nurses supply

  • Short supply of medicines, often pre-packed from unit stock, or a prescription for a community pharmacy
  • Day case: commonly painkillers

Nursing

ED / unit nurses (day case: recovery / day surgery nurses)

  • Cannula out, observations checked
  • Advice leaflet given
  • Patient and family told the plan
  • Day case: wound care and post-procedure advice

Admin

ED receptionists / ward clerks and clinic booking team

  • Discharge recorded on the hospital system
  • GP letter sent
  • Follow-up appointment booked

Logistics

Family or patient transport crews

  • Transport home
  • Day case: a responsible adult takes them home and stays overnight after general anaesthetic
All streams complete

Case 1 · Major trauma

Daniel, 30

Presentation
Motorcyclist hit by a car at speed
Route
999 pre-alert → ED resus → theatre → ICU (Level 3) → trauma ward → home
Length of stay
About 8 days, including 2 in ICU

Daniel arrives bleeding from a ruptured spleen with a broken thigh bone. He is too unstable for the surgical assessment unit, so he goes from resus straight to theatre. After surgery he spends two days in intensive care, steps down to the trauma ward and goes home once he is safe on crutches. His journey is high acuity but quick to discharge, because he is young and independent.

  1. 0–45 minScene and ambulance
  2. 45 min – 1 h 45ED resus
  3. 2 h – 5 hEmergency theatre and recovery
  4. Day 0 – 2ICU (Level 3)
  5. Day 2 – 8Trauma ward (Level 1)
  6. Day 8Discharged home

Highlighted boxes show Daniel's journey. Greyed boxes were not used.

Teaching point

Unplanned and high acuity, but short. The front door, theatre and ICU did the heavy lifting, and discharge was simple because Daniel needed no social care.

Case 2 · Chest infection

Mr Arthur Ellis, 84

Presentation
Confused and breathless at home; lives alone, walks with a stick
Route
999 → ED → AMU → respiratory ward → care home
Length of stay
About 19 days, 12 of them after he was medically optimised

Arthur has pneumonia. He improves on the respiratory ward and is medically optimised by day 7, but he has become weaker in hospital. A package of care at home is arranged and then judged unsafe, so he waits on the ward until a care home place is agreed. Most of his stay happens after he is medically ready.

  1. 0–1 hHome and ambulance
  2. 1 h – 6 hED
  3. 6 h – Day 1AMU
  4. Day 1 – 7Respiratory ward
  5. Day 7 – 19Medically optimised, waiting
  6. Day 19Moves to a care home

Highlighted boxes show Arthur's journey. Greyed boxes were not used.

Teaching point

Lower acuity but long. Twelve days in a hospital bed while medically ready is a delayed discharge: Arthur risks losing more strength and catching an infection, and the bed cannot be used for the next patient. This is where flow breaks down and where much innovation is aimed.
